How Rankine earth pressure is calculated

Rankine's theory treats the soil behind a retaining wall as reaching a state of plastic equilibrium. When the wall moves away from the backfill the soil expands laterally and the horizontal stress drops to its minimum value, the active pressure. When the wall pushes into the soil the horizontal stress rises to its maximum, the passive pressure.

Coefficients

For a vertical wall back, a horizontal ground surface and no wall friction, the coefficients depend only on the friction angle: Ka is the square of the tangent of forty-five degrees minus half of phi, and Kp is the square of the tangent of forty-five degrees plus half of phi. Kp is always the reciprocal of Ka for these assumptions.

Pressure at depth

Active pressure at any depth equals Ka times the effective vertical stress, minus twice the cohesion times the square root of Ka. Passive pressure equals Kp times the effective vertical stress, plus twice the cohesion times the square root of Kp. Cohesion therefore reduces active pressure and increases passive pressure.

The tension crack

In a cohesive soil the active pressure near the surface comes out negative, which would imply the soil pulling on the wall. Soil cannot sustain that tension, so a crack forms instead. This calculator sets any negative active pressure to zero and excludes that zone from the resultant, which is the conservative approach used in practice.

Water table

Below the water table the buoyant unit weight governs the effective stress, and pore water pressure acts on the wall separately as a full hydrostatic triangle. Water pressure is not reduced by Ka, which is why a poorly drained wall attracts far more total force than a drained one. The results above list the soil and water resultants separately for this reason.

Limits of the method

  • Assumes a vertical wall back, level ground surface and no friction between wall and soil
  • Passive values are ultimate; design practice applies a factor of safety
  • Sloping backfill and inclined walls need the Coulomb formulation instead
  • Results are for study and preliminary checking, not for construction without review by a licensed engineer

Converting an Asian transcript to GPA

Most universities in Taiwan, mainland China, Japan and Korea record marks out of one hundred. Universities in the United States, Canada and Australia expect something else, and the conversion is not standardised. Three different methods are in common use and they can differ by half a grade point on the same transcript.

Banded conversion

The most common approach in Taiwan maps score bands onto grade points: eighty and above becomes four, seventy to seventy-nine becomes three, and so on. It is simple but coarse, since a student averaging eighty-one and one averaging ninety-nine both come out at 4.0.

Linear conversion

The weighted average is multiplied by four and divided by one hundred. This preserves differences between strong students but tends to understate results, because an average of eighty-five converts to 3.4 rather than the 4.0 the banded method would give.

Weighted average mark

Australian universities, including the University of Western Australia and the University of Melbourne, mostly work directly with the weighted average mark rather than any four point scale. The calculation is the sum of each mark multiplied by its credit points, divided by total credit points. If you are applying to Australia this is usually the number that matters, and converting to a 4.0 scale first can only lose information.

Which number to submit

Check the admissions page of each programme before deciding. Many universities publish their own conversion table for specific countries, and where one exists it overrides every general method. Some require an official evaluation from a credential service instead. The figures produced here are for planning and self assessment.
